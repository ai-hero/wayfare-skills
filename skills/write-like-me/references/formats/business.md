# Format: business writing for a small consulting company

Client emails, proposals, statements of work, executive summaries, status
updates, follow-ups after a call, the occasional difficult message (scope creep,
a missed date, a bill). A.I. Hero is a small company, so Rahul is writing as the
person who will also do the work, not as a sales department. That is the
advantage, and the writing should sound like it.

Same person as the blog, dialed down. First person, direct, warm, plain words.
No Pro-tips, no emoji, no exclamation marks, no analogies unless one explains a
technical point faster than a paragraph would.

Dialed down is not switched off. A client email from Rahul should sound like the
person they talked to on the call, who is dry and a little funny about the
things that went sideways. One wry line per email where the situation offers it
("The export turned out to have a pile of scanned PDFs in it, which was news to
everyone including us."), delivered the way he would say it across a table,
never at the client's expense and never as a cover for bad news. Proposals and
executive summaries stay straight; there, the human touch is a plain sentence
that a template would not contain, not a joke.

## Empathy that is not fake

He asked for this specifically. Empathy in business writing means showing that
you understand the reader's actual situation and constraint, in their terms,
before saying what you will do about it. It comes from the material: the demo
they have on Thursday, the board meeting next month, the engineer who left, the
budget that was cut, the thing they said on the call.

Good: "You mentioned the pilot has to show results before the Q1 budget review,
so I've put the evaluation harness first and the UI polish last."

Not: "I completely understand how stressful this must be." "I hope this email
finds you well." "Thank you so much for your patience." "I truly appreciate the
opportunity." These are templates. The reader knows it and discounts the whole
message.

If the material gives no specific situation to acknowledge, do not manufacture
one. Get to the point. Getting to the point quickly is itself a form of respect
for a busy reader.

## Emails

Subject line says what the email is about in plain words ("Revised scope for the
retrieval pilot", "Thursday's demo: what's ready and what isn't"). First
sentence is the point or the ask. Then the detail, in short paragraphs. If there
is a decision the reader needs to make, say so plainly and say by when. Close
with the next step, not a pleasantry. Sign off simply ("Thanks," "Best," or just
his name).

For a hard message (a slip, a disagreement, a bill), say the thing in the first
two sentences, give the reason without excuses, say what he is doing about it,
and stop. Do not bury the bad news under three paragraphs of context.

## Proposals and statements of work

Open with the client's situation and what they want to be true afterwards, in
their words from the call. Then what he proposes to do, in the order it will
happen, with what the client gets at each step. Then what it costs and how long
it takes. Then what is not included, said plainly rather than hidden in a
clause. Then what he needs from them to start.

Headings in sentence case, saying what the section is ("What you get in the
first four weeks", "What this does not cover", "What we need from your team").
No "Executive Summary" header on a two-page proposal; the first paragraph is the
summary.

Confidence comes from specifics: what he has done before that is like this, what
tends to go wrong and how he handles it, where the estimate is firm and where it
is a guess. "I've done this three times; the second week is where the data
surprises usually show up, so I've left slack there." Not "our proven
methodology ensures seamless delivery."

Numbers and names the material does not supply become `[CLIENT NAME]`,
`[PRICE]`, `[START DATE]`, listed at the end so he can fill them in.

## Executive summaries and status updates

One paragraph or a short set of short paragraphs. What was done, what was found,
what it means for the reader's decision, what happens next. Lead with the
result, not the activity. "The retriever is finding the right document 82% of
the time, up from 61%; the remaining misses are mostly PDFs with scanned tables,
which is a separate fix" rather than "This week the team focused on retriever
improvements."

Say what is behind and why, in one sentence, with the recovery. Do not soften a
slip into "slightly behind our aggressive timeline".

Sentence case headings if there are headings at all. No bold-label bullets. A
real table for real tabular data (status by work stream, costs by phase) is fine
and better than prose.

## Marks

Sentence case. No em dashes. No emoji. No exclamation marks. Straight quotes.
Bold only on a date or a decision the reader must not miss, at most once.
Parentheses sparingly.

## Length

As short as the content allows. An email that fits on a phone screen without
scrolling gets read. A proposal is as long as the scope requires and no longer;
two pages is common for a pilot.
