# Format: LinkedIn post

Same person as the blog, in a space that gives him about 200 words before the
reader has to click "more". The reader is scrolling: a peer, a potential client,
a former colleague. They will give the first line two seconds.

## Shape

**First line does the work.** It is the claim, the surprising observation, or
the obstacle, in one plain sentence. No "Excited to share". No "I've been
thinking a lot about". No question that is really a setup for a sales pitch.
Something he actually believes and can defend in the comments. "Putting a human
in the loop slows the factory down. That's the point."

**Then one idea.** A post makes one point. If the notes have three, that is
three posts. Two to five short paragraphs, one to three sentences each, in his
conversational voice. The practical situation first (what someone did, what
happened), then what he took from it. First person for what he saw and did.
"You" when talking to the reader.

**End on something concrete.** The lesson, the preference with the reason, or a
real question he wants answered (not "Thoughts?"). An invitation to the comments
is fine if there is something to discuss. A link to the longer piece goes in the
first comment or at the very end, not in the first line.

## What stays and what goes

Stays: his opinions, contractions, a short verdict sentence, a rhetorical
question, "I prefer X because Y", one specific number or detail that proves he
did the thing, and one dry line. LinkedIn is full of people being solemn about
lessons learned; a post that can laugh at the situation (the agent that was
"done", the thing he should have set up on day one) stands out and sounds like
him. The light moment usually sits right after the fact it is about, as a short
verdict, not as a setup-and-punchline.

Goes: headers, bold-label bullet lists, hashtag walls (zero to three hashtags at
the end, if any), emoji as bullet points, the word "excited", "humbled",
"thrilled", the "Agree?" closer, and anything that sounds like a motivational
poster. Also the "I made a mistake and here's what I learned" template unless he
actually made the mistake in the material.

## Marks

No headers. No em dashes. No emoji (one at the very end is tolerable if he asks;
default none). Plain line breaks between paragraphs. Straight quotes. No
exclamation marks except where he would say the sentence out loud with one.

## Several posts from one piece

When turning a blog post or chapter into LinkedIn posts, each post takes one
section's point and the single best detail from that section. Do not summarize
the whole piece in one post; it comes out as a list of abstractions, which is
the thing he hates.
